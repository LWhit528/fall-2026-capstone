Artificial Intelligence Tools and Human Subjects Research

The Potential Role of AI in Human Subject Research

Introduction

This project investigated the use of AI in the creation of synthetic datasets to teach enduring concepts of data provenance, documentation, transparency, privacy, bias, validation, reproducibility, and responsible reuse.

We reviewed best practices, the pros and cons of synthetic data, created our own datasets as well as case studies for potential future workshops.

Literature Review
Pros/Cons of using synthetic data

Why use synthetic data?

  • Reduces exposure of sensitive data. Synthetic data allows students to practice working with data, creating visualizations, and analyzing trends without using actual student records.
  • Supports large datasets and collaboration. Tools such as LLMs and Python can generate large synthetic datasets quickly. Sharing the data, code, and generation methods can support open collaboration and reproducibility.
  • Creates opportunities to explore bias and specific scenarios. Carefully designed prompts can generate edge cases and varied scenarios for teaching. The resulting data still need to be checked for bias and unrealistic patterns.
  • Supports rapid testing. Fictional records can be used to develop and test analytical workflows before introducing real participant data.

Agal, S. (2026). A privacy preserving synthetic learner dataset for learning analytics in technology enhanced higher education. Scientific Reports, 16(1), Article 14772. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-44990-8

Why NOT use synthetic data?

  • Can reproduce or amplify bias. Synthetic data can reflect cultural biases and systemic inequities in the source data or generation process.
  • Cannot fully capture lived experience. Synthetic data may miss emotional complexity and contextual judgment that matter when interpreting human experiences.
  • Can be mistaken for evidence about real populations. Treating synthetic data as representative of actual students could lead to unsupported policy decisions or resource allocations.
  • May retain privacy risks. Depending on how the data are constructed, information about real individuals could be exposed or re-identified.

Case Studies for Classroom Use

Case Study 1: Student Mental Health and Academic Performance

A student wants to explore relationships between academic performance, stress, sleep, and mental health using AI-assisted statistical analysis.

Rather than collecting actual student records, the student generates a synthetic dataset of 500 fictional students using AI. The student plans to use ChatGPT or a university-supported AI tool to analyze patterns and create visualizations.

  1. Does this project require IRB review if only fictional data are used?
  2. What biases or unrealistic patterns might AI introduce into the dataset?
  3. Can the student publish findings based entirely on synthetic data?
  4. How should the student document and disclose AI use?
  5. What changes if the student later replaces synthetic data with actual student records?

Case Study 2: Student Success, Academic Records, and Mental Health

A faculty researcher is investigating how mental health affects academic success. The project combines:

  • Academic records: GPA, course performance, attendance, and academic standing.
  • Student interviews: Experiences with stress, mental health, and academic challenges.
  • Health records: Counseling or clinical records supplied by a HIPAA-covered healthcare provider.

The researcher wants to use AI to transcribe interviews, identify themes, and analyze relationships between academic and mental health data.

  1. Which data are protected by FERPA, HIPAA, or both?
  2. Which AI models and tools available at Colby would be appropriate for analyzing these data, and how do their privacy and security protections differ?
  3. What participant consent, IRB review, institutional approval, or legal agreements might be necessary?
  4. Could synthetic data be used to test the analytical workflow before introducing real records?
